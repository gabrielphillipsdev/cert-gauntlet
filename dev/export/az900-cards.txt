# AZ-900 flashcards
# Export for second-model review: FRONT / BACK / explanation. Flag any back or explanation that is factually wrong for the current product, citing Microsoft Learn.

[1] cat=cloud obj=1.1.1 (Define cloud computing)
FRONT: Cloud computing — the definition
BACK: #Computing services delivered over the internet | Compute, storage, networking, databases, AI — rented, not owned
WHY: Cloud computing means consuming compute power, storage and other IT capabilities over the internet from a provider's datacenters instead of buying and running the hardware yourself. The provider owns and maintains the physical infrastructure; you consume what you need and pay for that usage. AZ-900 opens with this definition, then builds every later topic (models, pricing, service types) on top of it.
HOOK: Somebody else's datacenter, your workloads, metered.

[2] cat=cloud obj=1.1.1 (Define cloud computing)
FRONT: Compute and storage — the two basic cloud building blocks
BACK: #Compute = processing power, storage = data at rest | Everything else (networking, databases, AI) layers on these
WHY: The study material frames cloud services as starting from two primitives: compute (virtual machines, containers, functions that run code) and storage (disks, blobs, files that hold data). Networking connects them, and higher-level services such as managed databases and AI are built from the same primitives. When an exam stem describes 'processing' it means compute; 'retaining data' means storage.

[3] cat=cloud obj=1.1.1 (Define cloud computing)
FRONT: Why the cloud provider, not you, runs the hardware
BACK: #Provider owns power, cooling, racks and physical network | You focus on the workload instead of the facility
WHY: A core promise of cloud computing is that the provider handles the undifferentiated work of running a datacenter: electricity, cooling, hardware refresh, physical security and the underlying network. That frees your team to concentrate on the application and data rather than the building. Exam stems that mention 'reduce the need to maintain physical infrastructure' are pointing at this benefit.

[4] cat=cloud obj=1.1.2 (Describe the shared responsibility model)
FRONT: Shared responsibility model — the idea
BACK: #Security duties split between provider and customer | The split depends on IaaS, PaaS or SaaS
WHY: The shared responsibility model divides security and operational duties between the cloud provider and the customer. The provider always secures the physical layer; the customer always owns their data and identities; everything in between shifts depending on the service type. AZ-900 asks you to place a given duty (patching an OS, protecting a database's data) on the correct side for a given service model.
HOOK: Provider = the building. You = your data. The middle moves.

[5] cat=cloud obj=1.1.2 (Describe the shared responsibility model)
FRONT: Shared responsibility — what the customer always keeps
BACK: #Data, devices and accounts/identities | These never move to the provider, even in SaaS
WHY: Regardless of service type, the customer retains responsibility for the information stored in the cloud, the devices allowed to connect, and the accounts and identities of people and services. Even with a fully managed SaaS application the customer decides who gets access and what data is uploaded. Exam distractors often try to hand data protection to the provider; it never goes there.

[6] cat=cloud obj=1.1.2 (Describe the shared responsibility model)
FRONT: Shared responsibility — what the provider always keeps
BACK: #Physical datacenter, physical network, physical hosts | The customer never touches these
WHY: The provider is always responsible for the physical datacenter, the physical network and the physical hosts the services run on. No service model hands these to the customer, because the customer cannot enter the building. When a stem asks who is responsible for the hardware a VM runs on, the answer is the cloud provider in every model.
HOOK: If it has a power cord, it belongs to Microsoft.

[7] cat=cloud obj=1.1.2 (Describe the shared responsibility model)
FRONT: Shared responsibility — the items that vary by service type
BACK: #OS, network controls, applications, identity infrastructure | IaaS: mostly customer. PaaS: shared. SaaS: mostly provider
WHY: Operating systems, network controls, applications and identity and directory infrastructure are the responsibilities that change hands. In IaaS the customer manages the OS and above; in PaaS the provider manages the OS and runtime while network and identity are shared; in SaaS the provider manages nearly everything except data, devices and accounts. A stem like 'who patches the guest OS of an Azure VM' resolves to the customer because a VM is IaaS.

[8] cat=cloud obj=1.1.3 (Define cloud models, including public, private, and hybrid)
FRONT: Public cloud — the model
BACK: #Provider-owned infrastructure shared by many customers | Accessed over the internet; no capital spend
WHY: In the public cloud model a third party such as Microsoft owns and operates the infrastructure and makes it available to any paying customer over the internet. Resources are multi-tenant at the physical layer, there is no up-front hardware purchase, and capacity is effectively unlimited from the customer's view. Azure itself is a public cloud. Exam signal: 'no capital expenditure', 'provider-owned', 'available to everyone'.

[9] cat=cloud obj=1.1.3 (Define cloud models, including public, private, and hybrid)
FRONT: Private cloud — the model
BACK: #Cloud used by a single organization | On-premises or hosted by a third party, but dedicated
WHY: A private cloud delivers cloud-style self-service and elasticity but the environment is used exclusively by one organization. It may live in that organization's own datacenter or be hosted and even managed by a third party, as long as the hardware is not shared with other tenants. The trade-off is higher cost and the organization still owns hardware lifecycle and capacity planning. Exam signal: 'complete control over the hardware', 'strict data residency'.

[10] cat=cloud obj=1.1.3 (Define cloud models, including public, private, and hybrid)
FRONT: Hybrid cloud — the model
BACK: #Public and private clouds connected and used together | Workloads and data can move between them
WHY: A hybrid cloud links a private environment (on-premises or dedicated) with a public cloud so that applications and data can span both. Typical reasons are keeping a regulated system on-premises while bursting or extending to Azure, or migrating gradually. Microsoft also describes multi-cloud (using two public providers) as a related option. Exam signal: 'extend the on-premises datacenter', 'keep some workloads local'.

[11] cat=cloud obj=1.1.4 (Identify appropriate use cases for each cloud model)
FRONT: Use case that points to a private cloud
BACK: #Strict regulatory or legacy constraints on where data lives | Organization must control the physical hardware
WHY: Choose a private cloud when regulation, contractual rules or legacy dependencies require that data never leave hardware the organization controls, or when the business wants cloud-style provisioning but cannot share infrastructure. Cost is higher and the organization still buys and maintains equipment. On AZ-900, the phrase 'must control the underlying hardware' is the tell for private.

[12] cat=cloud obj=1.1.4 (Identify appropriate use cases for each cloud model)
FRONT: Use case that points to a public cloud
BACK: #Avoid capital spend, scale quickly, pay only for use | No need to own or maintain hardware
WHY: A public cloud fits when the organization wants to stop buying servers, needs to scale up or down quickly, and is comfortable with the provider owning the physical layer. Startups, variable-demand web applications and anything where speed of provisioning matters are typical examples. Exam signal: 'minimize up-front costs', 'rapidly provision', 'no datacenter'.

[13] cat=cloud obj=1.1.4 (Identify appropriate use cases for each cloud model)
FRONT: Use case that points to a hybrid cloud
BACK: #Keep a regulated or legacy system on-premises, use Azure for the rest | Or migrate in phases
WHY: Hybrid is the answer when part of the estate must remain on-premises (a system tied to specialized hardware, a dataset with residency rules) but the organization still wants public-cloud elasticity for other workloads, or when it is migrating gradually and the two environments must talk to each other. Azure Arc and VPN Gateway or ExpressRoute are the tools that make the link. Exam signal: 'continue to use existing on-premises investment' plus 'take advantage of Azure'.

[14] cat=cloud obj=1.1.5 (Describe the consumption-based model)
FRONT: Consumption-based model — the idea
BACK: #Pay only for the resources you actually use | No up-front purchase, stop paying when you stop using
WHY: Under a consumption-based model the customer is billed for the resources it consumes (compute hours, gigabytes stored, transactions) rather than for capacity bought in advance. There is no minimum purchase and no wasted idle hardware, and spending ends when the resource is deleted. AZ-900 contrasts this with the traditional datacenter approach of estimating demand years ahead and buying for the peak.
HOOK: Like a utility meter: used it, pay for it; off, pay nothing.

[15] cat=cloud obj=1.1.5 (Describe the consumption-based model)
FRONT: CapEx versus OpEx
BACK: #CapEx = up-front spend on assets; OpEx = ongoing spend on services | Cloud shifts spending to OpEx
WHY: Capital expenditure is money spent up front on physical assets such as servers, network gear and datacenter space, which then depreciate. Operational expenditure is money spent over time on services as they are used. Cloud computing moves most IT spending from CapEx to OpEx because you rent capacity instead of buying it. Exam signal: 'reduce capital expenditure' or 'convert to operational expense' points to adopting a consumption-based cloud model.

[16] cat=cloud obj=1.1.5 (Describe the consumption-based model)
FRONT: Capacity planning in the cloud versus on-premises
BACK: #On-premises: guess demand, buy for peak, waste idle capacity | Cloud: match resources to real demand
WHY: A traditional datacenter forces you to estimate needs in advance; overestimating leaves hardware idle and underestimating causes slow performance and emergency purchases. In the cloud you add resources when demand rises and release them when it falls, paying only for the period they exist. This is why the consumption-based model and elasticity are presented together on the exam.

[17] cat=cloud obj=1.1.6 (Compare cloud pricing models)
FRONT: Pay-as-you-go pricing
BACK: #Billed on actual usage at list rates, no commitment | Most flexible, highest unit price
WHY: Pay-as-you-go is the baseline cloud pricing model: resources are metered and billed at standard rates with no long-term commitment, and you can stop at any time. It offers maximum flexibility at the highest per-unit cost. The pricing calculator defaults to this option, and reservations and savings plans are offered as discounts against it for predictable workloads.

[18] cat=cloud obj=1.1.6 (Compare cloud pricing models)
FRONT: Reservations and savings plans — the pricing idea
BACK: #Commit to one or three years for a discount | Reservation = specific resource; savings plan = hourly spend
WHY: Azure offers discounted rates when you commit in advance. A reservation commits to a specific resource configuration (for example a VM size in a region) for one or three years; a savings plan commits to an hourly spend on eligible compute and applies the discount flexibly. Both trade flexibility for lower cost on steady workloads. Exam signal: 'workload runs continuously for the next three years' plus 'reduce cost' points to a reservation or savings plan.

[19] cat=cloud obj=1.1.6 (Compare cloud pricing models)
FRONT: Spot pricing — the pricing idea
BACK: #Deep discount on spare capacity that Azure can reclaim | For interruptible batch work
WHY: Spot virtual machines use Azure's unused capacity at a large discount, with the condition that Azure can evict them when it needs the capacity back. They suit batch processing, rendering, test environments and other jobs that tolerate interruption. They are never the right answer for a workload that must stay available. Exam signal: 'interruptible', 'lowest possible cost', 'can be restarted later'.

[20] cat=cloud obj=1.1.7 (Describe serverless)
FRONT: Serverless computing — the idea
BACK: #Run code without provisioning or managing servers | Provider scales automatically; billed per execution/resources used
WHY: Serverless does not mean there are no servers; it means the provider fully abstracts them. You deploy code or a container, the platform runs it in response to events, scales it up and down (including to zero) and bills on what executes. Azure Functions and Azure Container Apps are the AZ-900 examples. Exam signal: 'event-driven', 'no infrastructure management', 'pay only while code runs'.
HOOK: Serverless = somebody else's servers you never see.

[21] cat=cloud obj=1.1.7 (Describe serverless)
FRONT: Serverless — the billing and scaling characteristics
BACK: #Scales automatically, including to zero when idle | Charged for executions and resources consumed, not idle time
WHY: The two properties that distinguish serverless from PaaS on the exam are automatic scaling to zero and execution-based billing. A function that receives no events costs nothing for compute, whereas a web app on a dedicated App Service plan is billed for the plan whether or not requests arrive. When a stem emphasizes sporadic workloads and minimizing cost during idle periods, serverless is the fit.

[22] cat=cloud obj=1.2.1 (Describe the benefits of high availability and scalability in the cloud)
FRONT: High availability — the benefit
BACK: #Service stays reachable despite component failures | Expressed as an uptime SLA percentage
WHY: High availability means a service continues to operate when individual components fail, typically by running redundant instances across fault domains, availability zones or regions. Azure expresses the guarantee as a service-level agreement (SLA) percentage such as 99.9 or 99.99 percent uptime. Exam signal: 'ensure the application remains accessible if a datacenter fails' points to deploying across availability zones.
HOOK: HA = more nines; each nine cuts allowed downtime tenfold.

[23] cat=cloud obj=1.2.1 (Describe the benefits of high availability and scalability in the cloud)
FRONT: Scalability — vertical versus horizontal
BACK: #Scale up/down = bigger or smaller instance | Scale out/in = more or fewer instances
WHY: Scalability is the ability to add or remove resources to match demand. Vertical scaling (scaling up or down) changes the size of an existing resource, such as moving a VM to a larger SKU. Horizontal scaling (scaling out or in) adds or removes instances, which is how scale sets and App Service handle traffic spikes. Exam signal: 'add more virtual machines' is scaling out; 'increase CPU and memory of the VM' is scaling up.

[24] cat=cloud obj=1.2.1 (Describe the benefits of high availability and scalability in the cloud)
FRONT: Elasticity — the benefit
BACK: #Automatic scaling in response to demand | Scale out at peak, scale in when quiet
WHY: Elasticity is scalability done automatically and in both directions: the platform adds capacity as load rises and removes it as load falls, so you neither over-provision nor pay for idle resources. Autoscale rules on scale sets and App Service plans are the implementation. On AZ-900, 'automatically' plus 'as demand changes' signals elasticity rather than plain scalability.

[25] cat=cloud obj=1.2.2 (Describe the benefits of reliability and predictability in the cloud)
FRONT: Reliability — the benefit
BACK: #System recovers from failures and keeps functioning | Decentralized global infrastructure makes this possible
WHY: Reliability is the ability of a system to recover from failures and keep working. The cloud supports this with a globally distributed infrastructure: if one region or zone fails, resources in another can take over. Microsoft lists reliability as one of the pillars of the Azure Well-Architected Framework. Exam signal: 'continue to function during a regional outage' maps to deploying in multiple regions.

[26] cat=cloud obj=1.2.2 (Describe the benefits of reliability and predictability in the cloud)
FRONT: Predictability — performance and cost
BACK: #Performance: autoscale and load balancing keep response consistent | Cost: forecast spend with Cost Management
WHY: AZ-900 splits predictability in two. Performance predictability means users get a consistent experience because autoscaling, load balancing and high availability absorb demand changes. Cost predictability means you can forecast and track spending using tools such as the pricing calculator and Cost Management. A stem that pairs 'predict' with 'budget' is talking about cost predictability.

[27] cat=cloud obj=1.2.3 (Describe the benefits of security and governance in the cloud)
FRONT: Security as a cloud benefit
BACK: #Provider handles physical security; you choose the control level | IaaS gives most control, PaaS/SaaS more built-in
WHY: The cloud improves security by having the provider protect the physical layer and by offering built-in capabilities such as DDoS protection, patching for managed services and threat detection. The customer picks how much control to keep: IaaS gives full control over the OS and software, while PaaS and SaaS hand more of the security work to the platform. Exam signal: 'protects against distributed denial-of-service attacks' is a built-in cloud security benefit.

[28] cat=cloud obj=1.2.3 (Describe the benefits of security and governance in the cloud)
FRONT: Governance as a cloud benefit
BACK: #Templates and policies enforce standards across every deployment | Compliance auditing and updates built in
WHY: Cloud governance means deployed resources follow corporate and regulatory standards, and the cloud makes this easier because templates define resources the same way every time and policy can audit or block non-compliant configurations. Azure Policy, management groups and ARM templates are the implementation. Exam signal: 'ensure all resources meet corporate standards' is a governance benefit.

[29] cat=cloud obj=1.2.4 (Describe the benefits of manageability in the cloud)
FRONT: Manageability of the cloud
BACK: #Autoscale, automatic monitoring and health, template deployment | Automatic replacement of failed resources
WHY: Management of the cloud refers to how you manage the resources themselves: automatic scaling on demand, deploying from preconfigured templates, monitoring health and replacing failed resources automatically, and receiving alerts based on metrics. These are platform features rather than tools. Exam signal: 'automatically replace a failed VM' or 'deploy identical environments repeatedly' point here.

[30] cat=cloud obj=1.2.4 (Describe the benefits of manageability in the cloud)
FRONT: Manageability in the cloud
BACK: #Portal, CLI, PowerShell, APIs and templates | Choose the tool that fits the task
WHY: Management in the cloud is about the ways you interact with Azure: the web-based portal, the Azure CLI, Azure PowerShell, REST APIs and SDKs, and declarative templates. All of them go through Azure Resource Manager, so the result is the same regardless of tool. AZ-900 treats the two senses of manageability (of the cloud versus in the cloud) as a pair; this card is the 'in'.

[31] cat=cloud obj=1.3.1 (Describe infrastructure as a service (IaaS))
FRONT: Infrastructure as a service (IaaS) — the service type
BACK: #Rent VMs, storage and networking; you manage OS and up | Most flexibility, most responsibility
WHY: IaaS provides the fundamental building blocks (virtual machines, disks, virtual networks) while leaving the operating system, middleware, runtime and applications to you. It is the closest to running your own datacenter and gives the most control, which also means the most patching and configuration work. Azure Virtual Machines are the canonical example. Exam signal: 'full control over the operating system', 'lift and shift without changing code'.
HOOK: IaaS = you get the hardware, you do everything else.

[32] cat=cloud obj=1.3.1 (Describe infrastructure as a service (IaaS))
FRONT: IaaS — who patches the guest operating system
BACK: #The customer | Provider patches hypervisor and hardware only
WHY: Because IaaS stops at the virtualization layer, operating system updates, antivirus, middleware and application patches are the customer's job. Microsoft patches the physical hosts and the hypervisor underneath. This is the most common shared-responsibility question on AZ-900: a kernel vulnerability on an Azure VM is the customer's to fix.

[33] cat=cloud obj=1.3.2 (Describe platform as a service (PaaS))
FRONT: Platform as a service (PaaS) — the service type
BACK: #Managed runtime; you bring code and data | Provider patches OS, runtime and infrastructure
WHY: PaaS adds a managed operating system, runtime and middleware on top of the infrastructure so developers can deploy applications without maintaining servers. The provider handles patching, scaling of the platform and availability; the customer manages the application and its data. Azure App Service and Azure SQL Database are the usual examples. Exam signal: 'developers should focus on code, not infrastructure'.
HOOK: PaaS = you get the stage, you bring the show.

[34] cat=cloud obj=1.3.2 (Describe platform as a service (PaaS))
FRONT: PaaS — the trade-off versus IaaS
BACK: #Less control over the OS and runtime versions | Far less operational work
WHY: Moving from IaaS to PaaS removes the ability to install arbitrary software on the host or tune the operating system, because the platform owns those layers. In exchange you stop patching, get built-in scaling and often pay less for the same workload. A stem that lists 'requires a custom kernel module' is steering you away from PaaS; one that stresses 'minimize administrative effort' is steering you toward it.

[35] cat=cloud obj=1.3.3 (Describe software as a service (SaaS))
FRONT: Software as a service (SaaS) — the service type
BACK: #Complete application rented per user or subscription | You manage data and access only
WHY: SaaS delivers a finished application that users access over the internet, typically on a subscription or per-seat basis. The provider runs everything from hardware to application code; the customer configures the product, manages users and owns the data. Microsoft 365 is the standard example. Exam signal: 'email service accessed from a browser', 'no software to install or maintain'.

[36] cat=cloud obj=1.3.3 (Describe software as a service (SaaS))
FRONT: SaaS — the customer's remaining duties
BACK: #Data, user accounts and access decisions | Everything else belongs to the provider
WHY: Even in SaaS the shared responsibility model leaves the customer accountable for the information it uploads, which users exist and what they can reach, and the devices allowed to connect. The provider cannot decide those for you. Exam distractors sometimes claim the SaaS provider is responsible for the customer's data classification or access reviews; that is wrong.

[37] cat=cloud obj=1.3.4 (Identify appropriate use cases for each cloud service type (IaaS, PaaS, and SaaS))
FRONT: Choosing IaaS, PaaS or SaaS — the decision rule
BACK: #Need OS control → IaaS; need to ship code → PaaS; need a product → SaaS | More control means more responsibility
WHY: AZ-900 use-case questions reduce to one question: how much of the stack must you control? If the workload needs a specific OS configuration, licensed software installed on the server or a lift-and-shift of existing VMs, choose IaaS. If the team writes code and wants to deploy without managing servers, choose PaaS. If the need is a ready-made application such as email or CRM, choose SaaS.

[38] cat=cloud obj=1.3.4 (Identify appropriate use cases for each cloud service type (IaaS, PaaS, and SaaS))
FRONT: Lift-and-shift migration — which service type
BACK: #IaaS | Move existing VMs without redesigning the application
WHY: Lift and shift (rehosting) means moving an existing workload to the cloud with no code changes, which maps to IaaS because the VMs, disks and networks are recreated as they were. Refactoring to PaaS or containers comes later if at all. Exam signal: 'migrate the existing servers as quickly as possible with minimal changes' points to Azure Virtual Machines, often with Azure Migrate doing the move.

[39] cat=arch obj=2.1.1 (Describe Azure regions, region pairs, and sovereign regions)
FRONT: Azure region — the definition
BACK: #Geographic area with one or more datacenters, low-latency linked | You pick a region when you deploy most resources
WHY: A region is a geographical area containing at least one, usually several, datacenters that are networked together with low latency. It is the unit you choose when creating most resources, and it determines where data lives, which services are available and what you pay. Some services are global (Microsoft Entra ID, Azure DNS) and do not take a region. Exam signal: 'deploy close to users', 'data residency' point to region choice.
HOOK: Region = where your stuff physically is.

[40] cat=arch obj=2.1.1 (Describe Azure regions, region pairs, and sovereign regions)
FRONT: Region pair — what it gives you
BACK: #Two regions in the same geography, updates staggered | One is prioritized for recovery in a wide outage
WHY: Microsoft pairs many regions within a geography so that some services can geo-replicate between them. Planned platform updates are rolled out to one region of the pair at a time, and if a disaster affects multiple regions, one region of each pair is prioritized for recovery. Pairing does not make your app resilient on its own; you still deploy to both. Many newer regions have no pair and rely on availability zones instead. Exam signal: 'East US and West US', 'replicated to a paired region'.

[41] cat=arch obj=2.1.1 (Describe Azure regions, region pairs, and sovereign regions)
FRONT: Sovereign region — the definition
BACK: #Physically isolated Azure instance for a specific government or jurisdiction | Azure Government (US); Azure operated by 21Vianet (China)
WHY: A sovereign region is an instance of Azure that is physically and logically separate from global Azure to meet a government's compliance and personnel requirements. Azure Government serves US federal, state and local agencies and their partners, with screened US personnel and dedicated datacenters; Azure in China is operated by a local partner. Eligibility is validated. Exam signal: 'US government agency' plus 'isolated instance'.

[42] cat=arch obj=2.1.2 (Describe availability zones)
FRONT: Availability zone — the definition
BACK: #Physically separate datacenter group inside a region | Own power, cooling and networking
WHY: An availability zone is a set of one or more datacenters within a region that has independent power, cooling and network, located far enough from other zones that a local failure (fire, flood, power loss) hits only one of them. Regions that support zones have at least three. Zones protect against datacenter-level failures but not against the loss of the whole region. Exam signal: 'protect against a datacenter outage within a region'.
HOOK: Zones survive a datacenter dying; regions survive a city dying.

[43] cat=arch obj=2.1.2 (Describe availability zones)
FRONT: Zonal versus zone-redundant services
BACK: #Zonal: you pin a resource to one zone (VM, disk, IP) | Zone-redundant: platform spreads it across zones automatically
WHY: Zonal services let you choose the specific zone for a resource, so you build resiliency by deploying copies in several zones yourself. Zone-redundant services replicate across zones automatically and fail over without your involvement; zone-redundant storage and many PaaS offerings work this way. A VM is zonal; ZRS is zone-redundant. Exam signal: 'automatically replicated across zones' points to a zone-redundant option.

[44] cat=arch obj=2.1.2 (Describe availability zones)
FRONT: Availability zones — what they do not protect against
BACK: #A full regional outage | For that you need a second region
WHY: Because all zones sit inside one region, a disaster or platform failure that takes down the region takes every zone with it. Zone deployment is the answer for datacenter-level resilience; multi-region deployment (often using a region pair) is the answer for regional resilience. AZ-900 likes to offer 'deploy to three availability zones' as a distractor when the stem says 'if the entire region becomes unavailable'.

[45] cat=arch obj=2.1.3 (Describe Azure datacenters)
FRONT: Azure datacenter — the physical layer
BACK: #Facility with racks of servers, power, cooling and network | Grouped into zones and regions; customers never choose one
WHY: Datacenters are the physical facilities that hold Azure's servers, with their own power, cooling, and networking. Customers cannot deploy to a specific datacenter; they choose a region or an availability zone and Azure places the resource. Microsoft owns and secures these buildings as part of its side of the shared responsibility model. Exam signal: 'physical security of the servers' is Microsoft's job.

[46] cat=arch obj=2.1.3 (Describe Azure datacenters)
FRONT: Datacenters, zones and regions — how they nest
BACK: #Datacenters form an availability zone; zones form a region | Regions group into geographies
WHY: The physical hierarchy on AZ-900 is datacenter, then availability zone (one or more datacenters), then region (one or more zones), then geography (a market such as United States or Europe that keeps data residency and compliance boundaries). The logical hierarchy of resources, groups, subscriptions and management groups is separate and should not be confused with this one.

[47] cat=arch obj=2.1.4 (Describe Azure resources and resource groups)
FRONT: Azure resource — the definition
BACK: #Any manageable item in Azure: VM, disk, VNet, storage account | Every resource lives in exactly one resource group
WHY: A resource is the basic unit Azure manages: a virtual machine, a disk, a virtual network, a storage account, a web app. Each resource has a type, a location and a resource ID, and must belong to exactly one resource group. Settings such as role assignments, policies and locks can target an individual resource or be inherited from its group. Exam signal: 'an instance of a service you create'.

[48] cat=arch obj=2.1.4 (Describe Azure resources and resource groups)
FRONT: Resource group — the definition
BACK: #Container that groups related resources for lifecycle management | Delete the group, delete everything in it
WHY: A resource group is a logical container that holds resources you want to manage together, usually those deployed, updated and deleted as a unit. Deleting a resource group deletes all of its resources, which is both a convenience and a hazard. Role assignments, policies and locks applied at the group are inherited by its members. Exam signal: 'manage as a single unit', 'remove all test resources at once'.
HOOK: RG = the folder; delete the folder and everything inside goes.

[49] cat=arch obj=2.1.4 (Describe Azure resources and resource groups)
FRONT: Resource group — the location and region rules
BACK: #Resources in a group can be in different regions | Group location only stores metadata; cannot nest groups
WHY: A resource group itself has a region, used only to store metadata about the group; the resources inside it can be deployed to any region, although keeping them together is recommended. Groups cannot be nested, a resource cannot be in two groups at once, and most resources can be moved to another group. Exam signal: 'can a VM in West Europe be in a resource group located in East US' is yes.

[50] cat=arch obj=2.1.4 (Describe Azure resources and resource groups)
FRONT: Moving a resource between resource groups
BACK: #Allowed for most resource types; subscription move also possible | Resource ID changes; some types have restrictions
WHY: Resources can generally be moved to a different resource group or even a different subscription in the same tenant, and the source and destination groups are locked during the move. Some resource types or dependencies block a move, so Azure validates first. Tags and role assignments on the resource itself travel with it; inherited settings from the old group do not. Exam signal: 'reorganize resources without redeploying'.

[51] cat=arch obj=2.1.5 (Describe subscriptions)
FRONT: Azure subscription — the definition
BACK: #Billing and access boundary that contains resource groups | Trusts exactly one Microsoft Entra tenant
WHY: A subscription is a logical container for resource groups that also serves as a billing unit and an access-control boundary. Every subscription trusts one Microsoft Entra tenant for identity, while a tenant can hold many subscriptions. Organizations create multiple subscriptions to separate environments, isolate billing per department or stay under per-subscription limits. Exam signal: 'separate invoices for each department' points to multiple subscriptions.

[52] cat=arch obj=2.1.5 (Describe subscriptions)
FRONT: Reasons to create more than one subscription
BACK: #Separate billing, separate environments, avoid quota limits | Each can have its own policies and roles

[86] cat=storage obj=2.3.3 (Describe redundancy options)
FRONT: Locally redundant storage (LRS)

WHY: LRS writes three copies of your data synchronously within a single datacenter in the primary region. [87] cat=storage obj=2.3.3 (Describe redundancy options)
FRONT: Zone-redundant storage (ZRS)
BACK: #Three synchronous copies across availability zones in one region | 12 nines; survives a zone outage, not a regional one
WHY: ZRS replicates data synchronously across three availability zones in the primary region, so an entire datacenter can fail and reads and writes continue. [88] cat=storage obj=2.3.3 (Describe redundancy options)
FRONT: Geo-redundant storage (GRS and RA-GRS)
BACK: #LRS in primary plus asynchronous LRS copy in secondary region | 16 nines; RA- adds read access to the secondary
WHY: GRS keeps three LRS copies in the primary region and asynchronously replicates to three more LRS copies in the secondary region hundreds of miles away, giving at least 99.99999999999999 percent durability. With plain GRS the secondary is used only after a failover; RA-GRS additionally lets applications read from the secondary endpoint at any time. Because replication is asynchronous, recent writes can be lost in a regional failover. Exam signal: 'protect against a regional disaster'.

[109] cat=idsec obj=2.4.5 (Describe Azure role-based access control (RBAC))
